Operating principle
An E.C. (electrical conductivity) probe measures the electrical conductivity in a solution.
It is commonly used in hydroponics, aquaculture and freshwater systems to monitor the
amount of nutrients, salts or impurities in the water.
Inside the conductivity probe, two electrodes are positioned opposite from each other,
an AC voltage is applied to the electrodes causing cations to move to the negatively
charged electrode, while the anions move to the positively electrode. The more free
electrolyte the liquid contains, the higher the electrical conductivity.

A conductivity probe is a very simple device. It is just two conductors with a fixed surface
area at a fixed distance from each other. This distance and surface area is known as the
conductivity cell. The cells distance and surface area is quantified as the conductivity cells
K constant.
How often do you need to
recalibrate a conductivity probe?
Conductivity probes work by measuring the electrical current of the water between two
graphite plates. The plates do not go bad, or change, so recalibration is not necessary. After
the first calibration your conductivity probe is good to go.

Operating principle
ORP stands for oxidation/reduction potential. Oxidation is the loss of electrons and
reduction is the gain of electrons. The output of the probe is represented in millivolts and
can be positive or negative.
Just like a pH probe measures hydrogen ion activity in a liquid; an ORP probe measures
electron activity in a liquid. The ORP readings represents how strongly electrons are
transferred to or from substances in a liquid. Keeping in mind that the readings do not
indicate the amount of electrons available for transfer.

An ORP probe is a passive device that detects a current generated from the oxidation or
reduction chemical substances in water. This current (which can be positive or negative)
is very weak and cannot be detected with a multimeter, or an analog to digital converter.
How often do you need
to recalibrate an ORP probe?
Because every use case is different, there is no set schedule for recalibration.
If you are using your probe in a fish tank, a hydroponic system or any environment that has
generally weak levels of chemical reactions you will only need to recalibrate your probe
once per year for the first 2 years. After that every ~6 months.
If you are using the ORP probe in batch chemical manufacturing, industrial process, or
in a solution that is known to have strong chemical reactions, then calibration should be
done monthly or in extreme cases after each batch.

Probe cleaning
Coating of the platinum tip can lead to erroneous readings including shortened span

be removed by vigorous stirring or by the use of a squirt bottle. Organic chemical, or

hydrochloric acid (HCl) soak for a few minutes, often removes many coatings. Do not use
abrasive materials on the ORP probe.

Operating principle
This galvanic dissolved oxygen probe consists of a HDPE membrane, an anode bathed
in an electrolyte and a cathode. Oxygen molecules defuse through the probes membrane
at a constant rate (without the membrane the reaction happens too quickly). Once the
oxygen molecules have crossed the membrane they are reduced at the cathode and
a small voltage is produced. If no oxygen molecules are present, the probe will output
0 mV. As the oxygen increases so does the mV output from the probe. Each probe will
output a different voltage in the presence of oxygen. The only thing that is constant is that
0mV = 0 Oxygen.

Operating principle
A pH (potential of Hydrogen) probe measures the hydrogen ion activity in a liquid.
At the tip of a pH probe is a glass membrane. This glass membrane permits hydrogen
ions from the liquid being measured to defuse into the outer layer of the glass, while
larger ions remain in the solution. The difference in the concentration of hydrogen ions
(outside the probe vs. inside the probe) creates a VERY small current. This current
is proportional to the concentration of hydrogen ions in the liquid being measured.

Coating of the pH bulb can lead to erroneous readings including shortened span (slope).

Because every use case is different, there is no set schedule for recalibration.

once per year for the first two years. After that every ~six months.

2.3 	Standard pH Buffer Solutions
Standard pH buffer solutions (buffers) are ionic solutions
that are used to calibrate the pH meter and electrodes. Buffers
maintain constant pH values (at a single temperature) because
of their ability to resist changes to the specific pH value for
which they are produced. However, the pH of solutions is
affected by temperature, because the calibration slope (which
is related to the Nernst equation) and the isopotential point
change with temperature. Modern pH meters are typically
calibrated with pH buffers having well-known pH-temper-
ature correlations; quality-assured and National Institute of
Standards and Technology- (NIST) traceable pH buffers are
available from USGS One-Stop1. Most pH meters recognize
the e.m.f. range of certain pH buffers (commonly pH 1.68,
4.01, 7.00, and 10.01 buffers) and are able to automatically
compensate for temperature during calibration. Verify that
the buffers you are using are NIST-traceable, that the values
are recognized correctly by the meter, and that the buffers are
compatible with the ATC for your meter. Consult the manu-
facturer’s manual to be sure that the meter will adjust the
expected value of the buffer for the temperature measured. The
user may be responsible for entering the temperature-adjusted
buffer pH value into the meter for calibration from a chart or
the meter may automatically generate a temperature-adjusted
pH value for the buffer.
Measurements of pH are only as accurate as the buffers
used to calibrate the meter and electrodes.
The commonly used pH buffer solutions and their corre-
sponding pH values from 0 to 90 °C are shown in table 6.4–3.
The pH buffers 1.68, 4.01, and 10.01 are primary standards
(pH values determined by using the standard hydrogen-gas
electrode). Their compositions are typically similar for various
manufacturers; therefore, their variations in pH with tem-
perature are well known. The pH-7.00 buffer is a secondary
standard (pH values determined by interpolation to primary
standards) and its composition (and therefore pH dependence
on temperature) may differ with the manufacturer. However,
the pH-7.00 buffer is typically made either from potassium
dihydrogen phosphate plus sodium hydroxide or from potas-
sium dihydrogen phosphate plus sodium hydrogen phosphate;
the pH values of both buffer formulations are comparable to
within 0.02 pH units from 0 to 90 °C.

5.2 	Measurement of pH in Waters with Low
Specific Conductance
Accurate and precise measurement of pH in low-specific-
conductance waters (less than 100 μS/cm) is challenging
because (1) the water is not sufficiently conductive to complete
the electrical circuit and allow for a stable pH reading using
a combination pH electrode, (2) differences in ionic strength
between the sample and buffers are large, and (3) the potential
of the liquid junction is larger than normal. Modern reference
junctions are designed to decrease the effects of low specific
conductance, but may not always produce the desired accuracy
for measurements of pH. Low-resistance glass pH electrodes
or reference junctions with a fast, continuous leak rate can
be used to make accurate measurements of pH in low-ionic-
strength waters; however, because these electrodes are expen-
sive, generally are not designed for fieldwork, and only work
well in dilute waters, they do not replace traditional electrodes.
Some methods recommend calibrating with low-ionic-strength
buffers to minimize liquid junction potential errors, but these
buffers are easily contaminated and should be avoided in
field settings.
The recommended method allows for using normal
pH field electrodes and standard pH buffers (table 6.4–3)
by adding a small amount of potassium chloride (KCl) to a
subsample of water to increase the specific conductance by
approximately 150 μS/cm. Increasing the specific conduc-
tance by this amount has little effect on the hydrogen ion
activity coefficient; the pH is expected to be affected by less
than 0.01 pH units (Busenberg and Plummer, 1987). It is first
recommended to determine the pH by using the procedures
described in section 5.1. If the sample pH does not stabilize,
and the specific conductance is less than 100 μS/cm, then the
following procedure is recommended:
1. 	Before going to the field, prepare small glass vials
containing 20 milligrams (mg) of dry potassium chlo-
ride (KCl—crystalline, American Chemical Society-
certified). Prepare more vials than the expected number
of pH measurements. (Note: Addition of other salts [for
example, sodium chloride] also will increase the specific
conductance, but KCl is recommended to minimize
effects on the liquid junction potential.)

raw sample water. Immediately transfer 20 mg KCl to
the sample solution, cap the solution to minimize trans-
fer of gases, and gently swirl the bottle to dissolve the
KCl. Maintain the sample at ambient temperature. This
procedure will increase the specific conductance of the
sample by approximately 150 μS/cm.

5.0 	Measurement of pH
Standard procedures for pH measurement are expected
to yield accurate sample pH measurements for waters with
specific conductance between 100 and 60,000 μS/cm, tem-
perature from 0 to 90 °C, and pH from 1.68 to 10. Some of
the procedures will need to be amended to accurately measure
pH at the extremes of these ranges, but standard pH calibra-
tions and combination pH electrodes can be appropriately
used to achieve an accurate pH measurement of environmental
waters whose characteristics fall within these ranges. Studies
of waters whose properties fall outside the ranges described
above (low-ionic-strength waters and [or] waters with pH less
than 1.68 or greater than 10) require further modifications of
pH calibration or measurement procedures.
The pH of sample water should be measured in situ or as
soon as possible after removal of the sample from its environ-
mental source. The pH of a water sample can change substan-
tially within hours or even minutes after sample collection
as a result of temperature change; changes in concentra-
tions of gases (carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, ammonia);
changes in redox conditions; mineral precipitation; metabolic
respiration by microorganisms; and other chemical reac-
tions, physical changes, and biological activity (Hem, 1989).
Field conditions, including rain, wind, cold, heat, dust, direct
sunlight, and direct exposure to vehicle exhaust, can cause
measurement problems. Always protect the instrument system
and the measurement process from the effects of harsh weather
and transportation damage.
In addition to potential changes in the composition of the
sample water, the reliability of pH measurements depends on
the accuracy of the calibration, and temperature measurement.
The physical limitations and characteristics of the electrode,
including response time, liquid junction potentials, and drift,
can affect the accuracy of the pH measurement.

Typically, the calibration should be done in a controlled

4.3 Measurement of Specific Conductance of a
Water Sample with Low or High pH
Specific conductance (and electrical conductivity) data
are reported at 25 °C. Most modern specific conductance
meters have the capability to compensate for temperature auto-
matically. Equation 2 is used most commonly to determine the
specific conductance at 25 °C and commercially available field
specific conductance meters typically use either a constant α
(typically 0.019–0.020 per °C) or a nonlinear α (International
Organization for Standardization, 1985). Whereas these com-
monly used temperature-compensation factors work well for
waters having a pH between 4 and 11, they can be the source
of errors as large as 50 percent for low-pH samples.
4.3.1 Measurement of Specific Conductance of
a Water Sample with Low pH (<4)
The specific conductance measurement error for low-pH
(<4) waters can be large if the commonly used temperature-
compensation factors are used. To determine whether an alter-
native temperature-compensation method should be used for
samples with pH <4, the in situ specific conductance should
be compared to the specific conductance of a sample equili-
brated to 25 °C. If the measurements are comparable (within
±5 μS/cm for specific conductance ≤100 μS/cm or ±3 percent
for specific conductance >100 μS/cm), then the temperature
algorithm used is likely sufficient. If a temperature-compen-
sation factor is required for a water sample with pH <4, the
method described by McCleskey (2013) is recommended.
4.3.2 Measurement of Specific Conductance of
a Water Sample with High pH (>11)
No temperature-compensation method is available for
waters with high pH (>11); therefore, the specific conduc-
tance should be checked by making a measurement at ambient
temperature and at 25 °C for waters with pH >11. At high pH,
hydroxide becomes a major ion in the solution and its mobility
is different than that of most other ions. However, the hydrox-
ide-equivalent conductivity is much lower than the hydrogen-
equivalent conductivity and, unlike in low-pH waters, the stan-
dard temperature-compensation approaches may be adequate.
Furthermore, relatively few natural waters have a pH >11, so
this situation is fairly rare.
