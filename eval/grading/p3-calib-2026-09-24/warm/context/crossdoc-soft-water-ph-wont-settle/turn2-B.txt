The reference junction in the combination electrode is
an electrically conductive bridge that completes the circuit
between the reference solution and the sample being mea-
sured. Typically, this junction is made of a porous material
such as ceramic, Teflon, or glass fiber. Glass capillary tubes
are also used in some electrodes. A properly functioning junc-
tion is important for accurate pH measurements.
Combination electrodes are housed in either a glass or
an epoxy body. Epoxy construction is recommended for field
applications for durability. Most systems today (2020) include
a thermistor for ATC. Depending on the pH meter, the thermis-
tor may be a separate probe or may be built into the pH elec-
trode, or, for multiparameter sondes, it may be incorporated
into a different part of the instrument.
Electrode performance degrades with normal use and
is required to be tracked in the pH-meter/electrode logbook
by changes in the calibration slope and isopotential point.
Use of the electrode in severe chemical environments can
cause more rapid deterioration (table 6.4–2). Many of these
environments coincide with industrial and urban locations or
waters that are highly acidic, have high concentrations of dis-
solved solids, or have high temperatures. If the pH electrode
is exposed to conditions listed in table 6.4–2, this informa-
tion should be recorded in the pH-meter/electrode logbook
and documented in field notes. Plan to replace the electrode
more frequently if it will be used in the conditions listed
in table 6.4-2. When measuring pH under specific adverse
conditions, use electrodes that are designed to operate under
those conditions (table 6.4–2).

5.2 	Measurement of pH in Waters with Low
Specific Conductance
Accurate and precise measurement of pH in low-specific-
conductance waters (less than 100 μS/cm) is challenging
because (1) the water is not sufficiently conductive to complete
the electrical circuit and allow for a stable pH reading using
a combination pH electrode, (2) differences in ionic strength
between the sample and buffers are large, and (3) the potential
of the liquid junction is larger than normal. Modern reference
junctions are designed to decrease the effects of low specific
conductance, but may not always produce the desired accuracy
for measurements of pH. Low-resistance glass pH electrodes
or reference junctions with a fast, continuous leak rate can
be used to make accurate measurements of pH in low-ionic-
strength waters; however, because these electrodes are expen-
sive, generally are not designed for fieldwork, and only work
well in dilute waters, they do not replace traditional electrodes.
Some methods recommend calibrating with low-ionic-strength
buffers to minimize liquid junction potential errors, but these
buffers are easily contaminated and should be avoided in
field settings.
The recommended method allows for using normal
pH field electrodes and standard pH buffers (table 6.4–3)
by adding a small amount of potassium chloride (KCl) to a
subsample of water to increase the specific conductance by
approximately 150 μS/cm. Increasing the specific conduc-
tance by this amount has little effect on the hydrogen ion
activity coefficient; the pH is expected to be affected by less
than 0.01 pH units (Busenberg and Plummer, 1987). It is first
recommended to determine the pH by using the procedures
described in section 5.1. If the sample pH does not stabilize,
and the specific conductance is less than 100 μS/cm, then the
following procedure is recommended:
1. 	Before going to the field, prepare small glass vials
containing 20 milligrams (mg) of dry potassium chlo-
ride (KCl—crystalline, American Chemical Society-
certified). Prepare more vials than the expected number
of pH measurements. (Note: Addition of other salts [for
example, sodium chloride] also will increase the specific
conductance, but KCl is recommended to minimize
effects on the liquid junction potential.)
2. 	At the sample site, fill a 250-mL polyethylene bottle with
raw sample water. Immediately transfer 20 mg KCl to
the sample solution, cap the solution to minimize trans-
fer of gases, and gently swirl the bottle to dissolve the
KCl. Maintain the sample at ambient temperature. This
procedure will increase the specific conductance of the
sample by approximately 150 μS/cm.
