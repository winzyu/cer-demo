## Turn 1

**Question:** We fill a bottle at the creek and read it on the bench meter back at the truck. How are we supposed to mix it before it goes into the little glass tube - is a good shake fine?

### Rubric

**Must contain**
- says not to shake the sample, because shaking beats air into it
- says to mix by turning the bottle end over end about 25 times, roughly one turn per second
- says to let the bubbles clear before filling the measurement cell
- notes that reading straight from the bottle into the cell is best avoided, and that if it cannot be avoided the sample is poured off immediately after mixing so the particles do not settle out

**Must not**
- recommends shaking, vortexing or stirring the sample
- invents a settling or rest time not supported by the source
- answers with the operator turbidity ranges instead of the handling procedure

**Should cite**
- usgs-nfm-a6.7-turbidity.pdf

### Answer A

For turbidity samples you should **gently invert the bottle about 25 times** to resuspend the particles, taking care not to create air bubbles. The guidance specifically says “Gently invert—do not shake—the sample 25 times … to completely disperse the solids, taking care not to entrain air bubbles”【5†"Gently invert—do not shake—the sample 25 times … to completely disperse the solids, taking care not to entrain air bubbles."】. Shaking can introduce bubbles that give a falsely high turbidity reading, so a careful inversion is the recommended mixing method.

### Answer B

You should gently invert the bottle about 25 times (roughly one inversion per second) to disperse the particles, rather than giving it a vigorous shake. The protocol specifically says to “gently invert—do not shake—the sample 25 times” and to avoid entraining air bubbles【3†"Gently invert—do not shake—the sample 25 times (ASTM"}. If you can’t invert, the guidance also notes to “invert the bottle 25 times using a 1‑second inversion cycle”【2†"invert the bottle 25 times using a 1-second inversion cycle"}]. This helps ensure a representative turbidity measurement without introducing bias from bubbles or uneven suspension.

## Turn 2

**Question:** And if we can't get to it until the next morning?

### Rubric

**Must contain**
- states that the sample must be held no longer than 24 hours
- states that it has to be chilled, at or below 4 degrees Celsius
- states that it goes in a clean amber bottle and is kept out of sunlight
- frames holding the sample as a fallback for when immediate measurement is not possible, not as normal practice

**Must not**
- invents a longer hold time or a different storage temperature
- says the sample can simply be refrigerated indefinitely
- loses the thread and answers about sensor storage or calibration-standard shelf life instead of the water sample

**Should cite**
- usgs-nfm-a6.7-turbidity.pdf

### Answer A

If you cannot read the sample right away, keep the bottle sealed and place it in a refrigerator (≈ 4 °C or colder). The guidance says to store the sample this way and then read it **within 24 hours**【5†"If you cannot read the sample within 30 minutes, store it in a refrigerator at 4°C (or cooler) and read it within 24 hours."】. After 24 hours a new sample should be collected.

### Answer B

If you must wait until the next morning, the guidance calls for short‑term storage under controlled conditions. Collect the sample in a clean amber bottle, keep it out of sunlight, and chill it to 4 °C or colder. The sample should then be measured within 24 hours of collection【1†"If temporary storage of samples is necessary, collect samples in clean amber bottles, keep out of sunlight, and chill at or below 4°C… The holding time must not exceed 24 hours"}]. This helps prevent biodegradation, growth, or particle changes that could bias the turbidity reading.
