Your dissolved‑oxygen (DO) readings of 6 – 11 mg L⁻¹ are well within the operator‑provided normal range of 5 – 14 mg L⁻¹, so the values themselves are normal.  
The daily swing you’re seeing is typical for a freshwater system: photosynthesis during daylight raises DO (and pH), while respiration and decomposition at night lower it. This diurnal pattern is a normal biological cycle and is expected to repeat each 24‑hour period unless a disturbance (e.g., a discharge, temperature spike, or turbidity event) alters the trend.  

If you notice the swing becoming larger, or if the DO drops below 6 mg L⁻¹, that could signal increased oxygen demand (e.g., organic loading). Otherwise, the 6 – 11 mg L⁻¹ range is healthy and expected.

## Turn 2

**Question:** Does pH do the same thing?

### Rubric

**Must contain**
- states pH and DO rise and fall together on the daily cycle
- explains the couple: photosynthesis removes CO2 and raises pH while releasing O2, respiration reverses both
- notes in-phase daily swings of pH and DO are the fingerprint of biological productivity

**Must not**
- claims pH and DO move in opposite directions on the daily cycle

**Should cite**
- water-quality-metrics-source-of-truth.pdf

### Answer A

pH is a different parameter than dissolved oxygen.  
It measures the acidity or alkalinity of the water (the hydrogen‑ion activity), not the amount of oxygen dissolved. While pH can vary from day to day, it is not a “swimmer” that oscillates like DO; it reflects the water’s chemical balance. In this deployment the acceptable pH range is 6.5 – 8.5 mg L⁻¹ (operator‑provided). If your pH stays within that window, it is considered normal.

### Answer B

pH can change, but it usually does not show the same daily “rise‑and‑fall” pattern that dissolved oxygen does.  While DO is strongly driven by photosynthesis and respiration that peak during daylight, pH is influenced by factors such as acid rain, runoff, and biological activity, which tend to vary more slowly or in response to specific events rather than a regular daily cycle.  In most streams, pH remains relatively stable unless a particular disturbance (e.g., acid rain, industrial discharge, or a sudden change in water chemistry) occurs.  Thus, the daily swings you see in DO are not typically mirrored by pH.

### Answer C

Yes—pH also shows a regular daily cycle, but it’s driven by the same biological processes that affect DO. During daylight, photosynthesis removes CO₂, raising pH (making the water more alkaline). At night, respiration adds CO₂, lowering pH (making it more acidic). This in‑phase daily swing is normal for freshwater systems and is part of the expected biological rhythm【5†L1-L8】【5†L19-L26】.

## Turn 3

**Question:** When should I be worried about it?

### Rubric

**Must contain**

**Must not**

### Answer A

You should be concerned when the pH falls outside the operator‑provided normal range of **6.5 – 8.5**.  
If the pH drops below 6.5 or rises above 8.5, it indicates that the water’s acidity or alkalinity is outside the acceptable window, which can stress aquatic life and affect other water‑quality parameters.

### Answer B

You should be concerned if the pH falls outside the operator‑provided normal range of **6.5 – 8.5**.  A value below 6.5 or above 8.5 can stress or harm aquatic life and may indicate a pollution source or changing water chemistry.  Even if the pH stays within the range, a rapid or consistent trend toward the extremes (e.g., a steady decline toward 6.0 or a rise above 8.8) warrants investigation, because it can signal an underlying issue that could eventually push the water outside the acceptable limits.

### Answer C

**When to be concerned about pH**

| Situation | Why it matters | What to look for |
|-----------|----------------|------------------|
| **pH falls below 6.5 or rises above 8.5** | Those values are outside the operator‑provided “healthy” range for freshwater (6.5 – 8.5 mg L⁻¹)【5†L19-L26】. | A sustained low pH can signal acid inputs (e.g., acid mine drainage, acidic runoff). A sustained high pH can indicate alkaline discharges (e.g., construction wash‑water, concrete runoff). |
| **Large or irregular daily swings** | Normal daily swings are modest and in‑phase with DO and temperature. Sudden, large jumps or erratic patterns may indicate a sudden input of acid or base, or a change in water chemistry. | Look for a step‑change that breaks the regular diel rhythm. |
| **pH changes that coincide with other parameter shifts** | pH is part of a multi‑parameter fingerprint. If pH drops while DO and ORP also fall, and EC rises, that pattern is typical of an organic or sewage discharge【5†L4-L7】【5†L4-L7】. | Correlate pH with DO, ORP, EC, and turbidity to confirm a pollution event. |
| **Persistent trend over days or weeks** | A slow, monotonic trend (e.g., pH steadily dropping over a week) may be sensor drift or a gradual change in water chemistry. | Check calibration history and compare to site‑specific baselines. |

**Bottom line:**  
- A normal, modest daily swing that stays within 6.5 – 8.5 mg L⁻¹ is expected.  
- Anything that pushes pH outside that range, or that shows a sudden, large, or irregular change—especially when other parameters shift in a consistent pattern—warrants further investigation.
